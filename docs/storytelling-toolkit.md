# Storytelling Toolkit

This toolkit defines the questions we'll ask when analysing episodes and characters.

## 1. Character and motivation

- What does the character want?
- What do they need, even if they don't recognise it?
- What beliefs, fears, or limitations shape their choices?
- What changes because of their decisions?

## 2. Conflict and stakes

- What is preventing the character from getting what they want?
- Is the conflict internal, interpersonal, social, physical, or philosophical?
- What can be gained or lost?
- How does the pressure escalate?

## 3. Story structure

- **Setup:** What does the audience need to understand?
- **Inciting incident:** What disrupts the current situation?
- **Turning point:** What changes the direction of the story?
- **Climax:** What moment concentrates the central conflict?
- **Resolution or hook:** What is settled, and what remains open?

These are useful lenses, not mandatory boxes. An episode may not contain every element.

## 4. World-building and exposition

- What rules does the story establish?
- How are those rules shown rather than explained?
- Which details make the world feel distinctive?
- Does the information arrive at a useful moment?

## 5. Themes and symbolism

- What recurring idea or question does the episode explore?
- What details support that interpretation?
- Could there be another reasonable reading?
- Is a symbol established by the story, or is it our interpretation?

## 6. Visual and audio storytelling

When evidence is available, consider framing, composition, movement, editing, facial expression, body language, music, silence, sound design, and action choreography.

Always explain how a technique affects the audience rather than merely listing it.

## 7. Foreshadowing and payoff

Separate:
- **Confirmed:** the story explicitly establishes this.
- **Suggested:** the episode provides evidence for this interpretation.
- **Speculative:** a possible theory that is not yet confirmed.

## 8. Evidence-led analysis

A strong analysis follows this pattern:

**Observation → Interpretation → Effect**

For example: identify a character's choice, explain what it reveals about their priorities, then discuss how it shapes the audience's understanding or moves the story forward.
